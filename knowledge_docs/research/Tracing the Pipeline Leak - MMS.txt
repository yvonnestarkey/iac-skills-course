Tracing the Pipeline Leak: A Reverse-Diagnostic of the Accounting
Student’s Mindset Meaning System
Abstract
With pass rates for key exit-level professional-accounting exams around 40 percent, many
aspiring accountants fail to qualify. This presents a ‘pipeline leak, and a paradox: students
who have successfully navigated years of coursework cannot navigate the final levels. This
paper argues that explanations relying on technical or skill deficits are insufficient. It utilises
the Mindset Meaning System (MMS) as a reverse-diagnostic lens to interpret this attrition
and to explain why traditional study advice may be ineffective for at-risk students, especially
given the increased focus on professional and transferable skills in assessments.
The paper employs a two-phase conceptual synthesis of accounting education literature.
Phase 1 identifies mindset-specific studies, noting a scarcity of research and a tendency to
sample successful students (capturing ‘untested optimism’). Phase 2 maps non-mindset
studies that explore accounting student challenges at higher levels in qualification journeys
against the four elements of the MMS: implicit beliefs, goal orientation, effort beliefs, and
failure responses.
The alignment of findings in accounting education with the elements of the MMS suggests
that accounting assessments may be filtering for psychological adaptive patterns rather than
technical or professional abilities. Observed maladaptive responses to setbacks - such as
surface-learning, burnout, and help-seeking avoidance - are traced back to performance-goal
orientations where students seek to validate, rather than develop, intelligence. The analysis
suggests that at-risk students’ underlying beliefs about intelligence may influence their
perception of and approach to the effort required to navigate higher levels of assessment.
Traditional academic interventions advising at-risk accounting students to emulate the study

approaches and habits of successful students may be ineffective. Incorporating academic
interventions designed to address underlying meaning systems may improve their learning

By reinterpreting fragmented findings through the MMS, this paper identifies research gaps
in accounting education research, including the lack of failure-based contexts and
performance-stratified sampling. It offers a theory-driven explanation for why traditional
academic interventions may be ineffective for at-risk students and for developing professional
and transferable skills, and suggests exploring students’ underlying meaning systems to
address challenges in the accounting pipeline.

1. Introduction
Pass rates for the final, exit-level professional-accounting exams remain persistently low. In
2024 and 2025, CPA pass rates for Financial Accounting and Reporting (FAR) and Business
Analysis and Reporting (BAR) were approximately 39 percent and 43 percent, respectively
(AICPA & CIMA, 2025). ACCA reported similarly low outcomes at its Strategic
Professional level, with Advanced Performance Management (APM) and Advanced Audit
and Assurance (AAA) recording pass rates in the 30 percent range across 2024 and 2025
(ACCA, 2025).
A significant portion of candidates will either repeat these exams or exit the pipeline at these
high levels —a phenomenon referred to here as the ‘pipeline leak’. This attrition presents a
paradox: these students have successfully navigated most of their academic journey,
demonstrating sufficient cognitive capacity and commitment to complete years of rigorous
coursework and assessments, yet they cannot pass their final exams. The assessments prior to
2

--- Page 3 ---

exit levels are intended to prepare students for those final exams or filter out students who
may not be capable of completing the qualification journey. The critical question, therefore, is
why accounting students who have proven their capability in earlier stages are unable to fulfil
the requirements at the final hurdles.
Accounting education research has explored various elements of this challenge, though
largely in isolation. Findings related to anxiety, burnout and intention to leave (Janse van
Vuuren, Bodenstein & Oberholzer, 2021; Fisher, Hageman & West, 2024; Smith, Haight,
Emerson, Mauldin & Wood, 2020; Khan, Mehmood & Huda, 2023), and inappropriate study
approaches (Jackling, 2005; Mashishi & Rabin, 2000; Natoli, Wei & Jackling, 2020) provide
valuable insight into the specific stressors that accounting students face. However, while
these studies document the symptoms of the pipeline leak, they provide no cohesive answer
regarding its root cause.
This paper suggests that these disparate concerns share a common psychological thread: they
are manifestations of a helpless response to challenges. In psychological literature, a helpless
response is not merely a lack of effort, but a maladaptive reaction to a perceived
unmanageable challenge, resulting in cognitive disruption, negative affect, and the
abandonment of effective strategies (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019).
When faced with unmanageable challenges1, student behaviours typically diverge into two
distinct patterns. Some students display mastery-oriented responses: they adapt to the
difficulty, seek help, and increase their effort, behaviours that significantly increase the
likelihood of achieving their final goals. Conversely, other students display helpless
responses: maladaptive behaviours that decrease the likelihood of achieving goals (Dweck &

Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019). The research question arising from this behavioural
difference is similar to the accounting pipeline paradox: “The puzzle, then, was why
individuals of equal ability would show such marked performance differences in response to
challenge.” (Dweck & Leggett, 1988). Research designed to answer that question gave rise to
the field of mindset research as we know it today (Diener & Dweck, 1978, 1980; Dweck &
Reppucci, 1973; Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019).
Given that the accounting pipeline leak represents a similar crisis of capability versus
performance, this paper applies the insights of that foundational research to the profession's
attrition problem. Specifically, we utilise the Mindset Meaning System (MMS), illustrated in
Figure 1. As a framework, the MMS suggests that a student's response to a challenge is not an
isolated event, but the final outcome of a cognitive chain—a dynamic meaning-making
process that influences how students interpret and respond to success and failure (Dweck &
Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019).
By viewing accounting student behaviours through the lens of the MMS, this paper integrates
fragmented findings of accounting research into a cohesive diagnostic framework. The
analysis proposes that the accounting profession pipeline is not merely filtering for technical
competence but is selecting for psychological responses.
This paper contributes three key insights: (i) A reverse-diagnostic framework which
establishes a cohesive lens that traces documented, at-risk behaviours—such as surface
learning, disengagement, and externalised responsibility—backward to their roots in the
Mindset Meaning System (MMS). (ii) Explanatory power for resistance, which provides a
theory-driven explanation for why at-risk accounting students often resist traditional support.
(iii) Strategic research gaps which identify critical blind spots in current literature to move
from conceptual observation to stress-tested intervention.

A natural starting point is to explore existing research on mindsets in accounting education.
However, research in this area is limited and is skewed towards students with little or no
experience of failure, leaving unanswered questions about how mindset-related beliefs
function among those most at risk of failure (Ravenscroft, Waymire, & West, 2012); Liu,
Robinson, & Xu, 2018; Beatson, Berg, & Smith, 2019; Farok & Mahmud, 2020). The MMS
as a dynamic meaning-making system remains unexplored in accounting education research.

2. Methodology
In this paper, the methodology plays a foundational role. It is necessary to outline the search
process, since the majority of the research in this paper relies on demonstrating the scarcity of
mindset evaluations in accounting education. Without establishing the limitations of the
existing literature, the reframing proposed in this paper could be misinterpreted as selective,
rather than a response to the nature of existing research in this domain.
5

--- Page 6 ---

The focus of this study is whether the MMS offers a coherent framework for interpreting
accounting students’ behaviours. It is conceptual rather than exhaustive, and the search
focused on identifying literature relevant to the four elements of the MMS. The
methodological approach for accounting education research included in this paper followed a
two-stage process.
First, locating accounting education studies that explicitly evaluate mindset and,
subsequently, identifying accounting education studies that—while not incorporating mindset
theory—reported findings consistent with the four elements of the MMS. The central purpose
of this paper is to consider whether the MMS offers conceptual value for interpreting
accounting students’ beliefs and behaviours; therefore, both stages were required.
2.1 Phase 1: Mindset-specific studies within accounting education
The search began with the narrowest possible scope: studies that directly measured
accounting students’ mindsets. Searches included Google Scholar, Scopus and high-ranking
accounting education journals. LitMaps and AI semantic search tools (Elicit and ChatGPT)
were then used, which identify conceptually related work rather than relying solely on
keyword matches.
‘Mindset’ keyword searches regularly return research that uses the term generically (e.g.,
digital mindset, leadership mindset, entrepreneurial mindset) or mentions growth mindsets as
a desirable trait in accounting studies and careers, but does not evaluate mindsets.
Bibliographies of mindset-specific papers were reviewed to determine whether any additional
accounting mindset studies existed. None appeared.
Only a small group of studies in accounting education have conducted theoretically grounded
mindset evaluations (Annexure 1).

To enhance transparency, the search process was recorded in a publicly accessible video
(Vimeo Link). The recording shows the output of each search tool and confirms that few
studies directly evaluate mindset in accounting education.
2.2 Phase 2: Accounting studies aligned with MMS components
The second stage expanded the scope to include studies that, although not incorporating
mindset theory, reported findings aligned with the MMS elements (Figure 1). These studies
(Annexure 2) identify narratives and behavioural patterns that align with mindset-related
constructs. LitMaps and Elicit were particularly helpful for locating conceptually adjacent
studies.
2.3 Scope decision
This paper focuses on the paradox of students at higher levels in their academic journey.
Many widely cited studies on accounting education are based on first-year student samples.
These cohorts often include many students who take an accounting introduction course as
part of other degrees. Their challenges, academic trajectories and lack of evidence-based
ability in the accounting pipeline differ markedly from those of exit-level students. To
maintain conceptual consistency in interpreting belief–behaviour patterns, many first-year
studies were excluded unless they evaluated mindset or offered qualitative insight.
3. Mindset Meaning System Introduction
Early investigations by mindset researchers into helpless and mastery-oriented responses to
setbacks and failure evolved into a broader framework, the Mindset Meaning System (MMS)
(Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019; Figure 1).
Consider two students, ‘A’ and ‘B’, both of whom are preparing for their final professional
7

--- Page 8 ---

exam. As part of their preparation, they take a mock exam, and both fail. Student A goes on
to pass the final exam, while Student B fails. Initial perceptions may be that Student B lacks
the technical competence required to pass at that level.
Attempts to support Student B will frequently focus on study techniques or increased effort,
and it is common to advise them to emulate Student A’s approaches to meet the
qualification’s academic requirements.
Mindset research suggests, however, that if the students had different interpretations of their
mock exam failure, based on different beliefs about intelligence and learning, this would have
affected their exam preparation and, in turn, their final results (Dweck & Leggett, 1988;
Dweck, 2006; Dweck & Yeager, 2019; Cha, Kim, Lee, & Anderman, 2025). Students A and
B may have identical levels of technical ability, but their interpretations of challenging
situations will affect their responses and, in turn, their results.
The MMS proposes that implicit beliefs about intelligence predict goal orientation and effort
beliefs, which in turn dictate the students’ response to setbacks and failure (Dweck & Yeager,
2019; Blackwell, Dweck, & Trzesniewski, 2007; Cha et al., 2025; Burnette, O’Boyle,
VanEpps, Pollack, & Finkel, 2013). Table 1 illustrates the MMS for Students A and B, using
narrative examples to show how their beliefs would practically affect their interpretations of
and responses to the failure. This explains why classic study advice may be sound, but
Student B may not adopt it.
This paper refers to Students A and B as ‘growther’ and ‘fixeder’, respectively. The terms "
growther and fixeder will be used in this paper to refer to students displaying growth and
fixed-mindset behaviours. This is not an assumption about the mindset beliefs of accounting
students. We explore research suggesting that accounting students display fixed-mindset
behaviours, but further research will be required to obtain mindset evaluations that provide

More recent research suggests that mindsets are more nuanced and less directly correlated
with academic performance (Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler & Macnamara, 2018; Macnamara
& Burgoyne, 2023; Cha et al., 2025). Regardless of mixed findings, all studies agree that
growth mindsets are desirable for students; their concern is often whether there is sufficient
correlation with academic performance to warrant allocating resources to interventions.
The sections below represent a reverse-diagnostic approach to the MMS elements. It begins
by examining responses to setbacks and challenges, then traces these behaviours back to their
cognitive precursors—effort beliefs and goal orientations—and to the underlying implicit
beliefs that are suggested to create these divergences.
4. Responses to Setbacks and Failure
The MMS suggests that students’ responses to setbacks and failure are observable outcomes
of their underlying beliefs and the behaviours they adopt in response (Figure 1; Dweck &
Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019).
This section will explore several studies in accounting education whose findings align with
helpless responses, specifically regarding ineffective learning strategies and various forms of
10

--- Page 11 ---

disengagement (Annexure 2). While these studies were not developed with mindset theory in
mind, the observed outcomes provide valuable insights into at-risk students’ responses to
failure.
4.1 Inappropriate Learning Strategies
To navigate the increasing complexity of the accounting curriculum, students must adapt their
learning strategies. The MMS views this type of adaptation as a mastery-oriented response.
Conversely, the inability or unwillingness to adjust learning strategies despite increasing
demands is viewed as a helpless response, often leading to a deterioration of problem-solving
skills (Dweck, 2006; Diener & Dweck, 1978, 1980; Dweck & Yeager, 2019). Students
displaying helpless responses were later found to be more likely to have fixed-mindset beliefs
(Dweck & Leggett, 1988). Interestingly, Dweck and Leggett (1988) found that fixeders had
often performed better in tasks than their growther peers before they experienced
unmanageable challenges, again contradicting the belief that lower ability was the cause of
helpless responses.
Qualitative studies of at-risk and failing accounting students report a specific inability to
manage time or adopt appropriate study methods, even when current strategies are clearly
failing (Eiselen & Geyser, 2004; Gracia & Jenkins, 2002).
Mashishi and Rabin (2000) observed that fourth-year accounting students predominantly
relied on surface learning, despite lecturer expectations for deep learning at this level in their
qualification journey. Crucially, every student in this sample had passed the previous three
years on their first attempt. This profile—academic success followed by a failure to adapt to
meet requirements—is a hallmark of the accounting pipeline problem.
The negative perceptions of teaching quality reported by Jackling (2005) and Natoli et al.
(2020) were associated with surface learning approaches. In the latter study, more than 90
11

--- Page 12 ---

percent of students rated most aspects of the course experience negatively, and a similar
proportion (91.9 percent) adopted a surface learning approach.
Interestingly, Jackling (2005) found that this was often a calculated response by students -
They believed assessment tasks primarily required memorisation and therefore utilised
surface strategies to meet those demands. This approach was ultimately ineffective for
genuine learning, as students with predominantly surface motives generally failed to answer
conceptual questions during interviews, demonstrating a clear lack of deep understanding.
Similarly, AlBassam, AlAwadi and Alrashed (2019) observed a strong dependence on
lecturer-led delivery, which correlated with weak judgment and limited self-directed study.
The recurrence of these behaviours raises a question of causality: Do students adopt surface
approaches because teaching is poor? Do they perceive teaching as poor because they lack
the ownership required for deep learning? Or do they adopt surface approaches because the
strategy works at earlier levels, and when they no longer yield results, they blame the
teaching quality for not adequately preparing them and struggle to adapt?
Ravenscroft et al. (2012) included mindset, calibration error, and academic performance in
their study. They hypothesised that growthers would exhibit lower calibration errors and
would have higher academic performance across the tests. Counterintuitively, a growth
mindset predicted improvement on only one of the three tests. While the study measured how
well students could predict their results, it did not measure how they interpreted those results
or how they responded to those interpretations. Thus, it remains unclear whether the
inconsistent relationship reflects differences in effort, strategy adaptation, or emotional
responses to prior performance.
The moderate levels of grit displayed by 75 percent of the accounting students in Farok and
Mahmud’s (2020) study failed to translate into academic performance. Similarly, the
12

growthers who prioritised the Big Four firms' goals in Liu et al.’s (2018) study performed
worse on their final exams. This suggests that their effort was not aligned with the required
outcomes of the assessments or course requirements.
4.2 Disengagement - Burnout, Anxiety, Withdrawal
In mindset research, helpless students often withdraw cognitively, emotionally, or
behaviourally when they perceive a task as unmanageable or when effort seems unlikely to
yield improvement (Dweck & Yeager, 2019; Dweck, 2006; Diener & Dweck, 1978, 1980;
Figure 1).
Fixeders show greater vulnerability to dysphoria (the acute distress arising from a perceived
gap between one’s current state and an 'ideal' self), and are less effective at coping once they
experience dysphoria, than growthers (Baer, Grant, & Dweck, 2005; Dweck, 2006), with one
study reporting increasing depression levels for fixeders due to continuous “ruminating over
their problems and setbacks, essentially tormenting themselves with the idea that the setbacks
meant they were incompetent or unworthy”. The more depressed they felt, the less work they
did. While the study found that growthers were struggling with depression as well, they took
action to confront their problems (Dweck, 2006).
We see evidence of depression, burnout and anxiety in accounting education. Fisher et al.
(2024) found that more than 85 percent of accounting majors reported burnout, identifying
high levels of cognitive test anxiety - a debilitating state where students 'freeze up' during
exams despite preparation. They also measured disengagement as a core component of
burnout, finding that students displayed modest to high levels of withdrawal from their
studies. Crucially, the authors found that these helpless responses were predicted by
maladaptive perfectionism—the tendency to equate mistakes with a loss of self-worth.
13

--- Page 14 ---

These findings suggest that the freezing response and the disengagement are triggered by a
specific underlying belief system regarding failure and identity, a relationship that aligns with
the MMS and a meta-analysis of 113 broader mindset research papers (Burnette et al. 2013).
The severity of this helpless response is underscored by Smith et al. (2020), who found that
role stressors—specifically role ambiguity and role overload-significantly decreased
accounting students' psychological health and increased academic burnout, which in turn
drove intentions to depart the major entirely. Although not a mindset study, the authors draw
on mindset research (Yeager & Dweck, 2012) to argue that resilience is malleable. Their
findings support this, showing that higher resilience acted as a protective mechanism, directly
enhancing psychological health and reducing the burnout that leads to withdrawal.
Consequently, Smith et al. (2020) posit that resilience training would be valuable for reducing
burnout and departure intentions among accounting students.
Significant predictors of accounting students’ academic burnout and the intention to leave the
institution include low grit (Khan et al., 2023) and a combination of high cynicism and low
emotional well-being (Van den Berg & Rothmann, 2024).
Van den Berg and Rothmann (2024) define cynicism not merely as negativity, but as a
‘distant attitude’ stemming from a perceived ‘lack of efficacy and control’ over educational
outcomes. This psychological detachment serves as a defence mechanism for students who
feel their capabilities are insufficient for the demands of the curriculum.
Janse van Vuuren et al. (2021) reported notably high levels of anxiety and depression among
postgraduate accounting students, accompanied by low absorption2 and low vigour during
demanding periods. Anxiety and depression were also positively correlated, and among

There are other, more subtle forms of helpless responses. Early mindset studies, for example,
found that students frequently cited boredom or a sudden loss of interest as excuses for their
failure when tasks became difficult, in an attempt to shield their self-esteem from the
implication of inability (Dweck & Leggett, 1988). This mirrors the qualitative findings of
Molendijk, Taplin and Brennan (2024), who found that ‘boredom’ and ‘too much
information’ were leading causes of disengagement among accounting students.

‘interesting’ was also the leading cause of engagement, with ‘detail’ generally cited as a
reason for increased engagement. Thus, the same concept is interpreted both positively and

negatively by different students. Through an MMS lens, the reasons for disengagement may

There is a common saying: “It is better to have tried and failed than never to have tried at
all”. This is not a belief endorsed by fixeders, whose response to perceived or possible failure
may be to avoid it by removing themselves from the situation before it happens. As noted in
early studies on mindset, protecting their self-identity makes them unlikely to cite a
deficiency in their intelligence as the reason. Rather, they claim a loss of interest, a change of
goals, or lay external blame (Dweck, 2006; Diener & Dweck, 1978, 1980; Dweck & Leggett,
1988).
4.3 Confidence
Research on mindset suggests that fixeders experience a decline in confidence after failure
(Dweck & Henderson, 1990) and that confidence is a moderating factor influencing whether
a fixeder will display mastery-oriented or helpless responses to failure.
Beatson et al. (2019)3, however, reported no decline in confidence among fixed-mindset
accounting students after setbacks. Several design features, however, limit the study’s
relevance for interpreting accounting students’ failure responses using the MMS.
Confidence was assessed only once at the start of the semester, and most students passed the
course comfortably, so there was no reassessment of confidence after results were obtained
and no meaningful failure to trigger a decrease in confidence. Only 20 percent of the sample
was identified as having a fixed mindset, yet conclusions about the mindset–confidence

4.4 Synthesis
Few of the accounting education studies discussed here explicitly categorise their findings by
performance. The omission leaves a critical question unanswered: are these patterns triggered
or amplified by academic difficulty or failure?
Despite the lack of performance data, the patterns observed in accounting education studies
align with the MMS’s theory of helpless responses to a highly challenging study
environment.
The evidence challenges the assumption that pipeline attrition is merely the natural filtration
of less capable candidates. Instead, it implies that the pipeline may be selecting for
psychological responses rather than ability: when encountering difficulty, students with
mastery-oriented responses adapt and progress, while those exhibiting helpless
responses—regardless of their prior competence—utilise inappropriate study approaches or
disengage.
Within the MMS, effort belief dictates whether a student displays helpless responses to
failure. If a student withdraws when tasks become demanding, it implies a maladaptive
relationship with effort. The following section examines this construct of effort beliefs.

5. Effort Beliefs
According to mindset research, effort belief is an MMS element that dictates students’
responses to failure. A question raised here is whether the helpless responses we see in
accounting students may be influenced by a belief that the need to put in more effort signals
lower ability and that effort will not change the outcome (Dweck & Sorich, 1999; Dweck &
Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019).
This interpretation of effort belief aligns with broader motivational research. Nicholls (1984),
Folmer, Cole, Sigal, Benbow, Satterwhite, Swygert and Ciesla (2008), and Miele, Browman
and Vasilyeva (2020) show that students often associate high ability with low effort,
particularly as they progress through schooling.
While accounting education research often explores effort in terms of its volume and type,
few studies examine the beliefs that underpin accounting students’ decisions about effort.
A common misconception is that fixeders are effort-averse. This is an oversimplification that
misrepresents the belief-response mechanism of mindset beliefs and will be clarified here.
5.1 The Futility of Effort
A growther views ability as a moving target. A person may build the ability to perform a task
tomorrow that they are not capable of achieving today, even if it feels impossible at the time
(Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019). Academic resources and interventions are
effective for this student when they are struggling with their studies because they are seeking
a bridge from ‘cannot’ to ‘can’ and believe that their effort will make a difference in their
results, even if it does not seem positive yet.
For the fixed-mindset student, however, ability is perceived as static. If a person is intelligent,
they will have the ability to achieve the task fairly easily. Inability to achieve the task,
18

--- Page 19 ---

therefore, signals an intelligence deficiency in that area, and since intelligence is perceived as
static, the inability is perceived as permanent (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager,
2019). A person either cannot do something, or they can. There is no bridge between the two.
For a fixeder, no academic resources, advice or comparisons to successful students and their
habits will persuade them to invest effort if they believe that effort is futile and will not
change their abilities. Academic resources and interventions for this student will be less
effective when they are struggling, since their logic suggests that if they were capable of
doing it, the struggle or additional effort would be unnecessary. This perception of futility is
observable across accounting education research.
Eiselen and Geyser (2004) found that at-risk accounting students—those who stood to benefit
most—avoided academic support tools, suggesting they believed remediation would not alter
their trajectory. Similarly, the reduced vigour reported by postgraduate students in Janse van
Vuuren et al. (2021) suggest a depleted capacity to invest effort when the perceived return is
low.
This mirrors Jackling’s (2005) findings, where students reverted to surface learning in
response to high workloads, effectively calculating that the 'cost' of deep effort outweighed
the likelihood of success. Collectively, these patterns align with the fixed-mindset view that
increased effort is both unappealing and unpromising
5.2 The Distinction Between Effort and Struggle
To understand the pipeline leak, it is crucial to distinguish between effort and struggle. When
effort directly translates into results, and each additional ‘unit’ of studying (e.g., hours,
summaries, classes, tutorials) increases those results, this represents effort but no struggle.
When the effort does not translate to results, the student spends more hours, but their marks
19

--- Page 20 ---

do not increase, or worse, they decrease - This represents struggle. Mindset research suggests
that fixeders are as willing to put in effort as growthers before failures and setbacks (Hong,
Chiu, Dweck, Lin & Wan, 1999; Cha et al., 2025). Thus, it is only when effort does not
translate into results that fixed-mindset students begin to withhold effort, believing that effort
will not be the differentiating factor between what they can and cannot do.
This means we cannot interpret a student’s investment of effort as evidence of resilience or as
a predictor of future persistence, since their effort decisions may change when they encounter
an unmanageable challenge (Hong et al. 1999; Cha et al. 2025).
The MMS framework suggests a distinct profile for the pipeline leak candidate: a student
who invested effort and achieved commensurate results throughout early studies, but who
lacks the belief structure to navigate the non-linear demands of professional qualification.
When these students encounter the higher-level hurdles, the requirement for a different kind
of effort—one that involves struggle and ambiguity—is interpreted through a fatalistic lens.
Their belief system suggests that this struggle is not a part of the learning process, but a
signal that they have reached the absolute limit of their intelligence. They believe that if they
were truly ‘accountant material,’ it would not be this hard.
Accounting education studies that include mindset evaluations refer to effort beliefs but do
not explore them (Ravenscroft et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2018; Beatson et al., 2019; Farok &
Mahmud, 2020), thus we do not have insight into these students’ beliefs about effort.
Additionally, the lack of performance stratification in many accounting education studies
obscures shifts in effort beliefs.
Incorporating effort-belief measures (Blackwell et al., 2007; Sigmundsson & Haga, 2024)
would enable future accounting education research, particularly research involving at-risk
20

--- Page 21 ---

students, to distinguish between students who struggle because they lack effective strategies
and those who struggle because they interpret effort as futile.
While effort belief concerns students’ interpretations of effort and intelligence during
studying, an important question the MMS asks is: What is the students’ perception of the
purpose of their studying - their goal orientation?
6. Goal Orientation
The MMS draws from achievement goal theory (Ames, 1992) and suggests that goal
orientations also dictate students’ responses to failure.
Fixeders are generally oriented toward performance goals (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck
& Yeager, 2019; Figure 1). Since they view intelligence as a fixed trait, their primary concern
becomes documenting and validating their high level of it. Their goal is ‘looking smart’ or
avoiding ‘looking dumb’.
Growthers are generally oriented toward learning goals. Since they believe ability can be
developed, their primary concern is increasing their competence. Their goal is to acquire new
skills or master tasks (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Blackwell et al., 2007; Cha et al., 2025;
Burnette et al., 2013; Dweck & Yeager, 2019).
Goal achievement theory is nuanced, and research acknowledges more complex relationships
between goal types and performance (Grant & Dweck, 2003). While these complexities are
recognised, the simpler distinction between learning and performance goals offers a valuable
lens here for interpreting accounting students’ behaviour, particularly when performance
pressure is high.
At-risk accounting students in Eiselen and Geyser (2004) who dismissed prior accounting
21

--- Page 22 ---

learning as a ‘waste of time’ because it had not been sufficient to pass the current level
indicate a performance-goal lens. Performing students, on the other hand, recognised that
prior learning was a foundation they needed to build on, reflecting a belief that the purpose
and process of studying are cumulative.
One accounting education paper incorporating mindset theory (Liu et al., 2018) reported that
growthers held stronger aspirations to work for a Big Four firm after their studies. The
authors suggest that, as growthers, they perceive intelligence to be malleable, are less afraid
of failure, and thus are more willing to set highly challenging, competitive career goals.
While this suggests that the study included goal orientation from the MMS, they actually
refer to ‘goal-setting theory’ (Locke & Latham, 1990), which focuses on what the student
wants to achieve rather than the underlying goal that directs their learning.
6.1 Academic Evaluative Sensitivity
If the maintenance of the appearance of competence is more important to the fixeder than
developing competence, any situation that may be considered ‘evaluative’ will be of concern
to them, even if they are routine academic practices not related to final grades, and even if it
is during the ‘learning’ phase of the academic period as opposed to the ‘assessment’ phase.
Blondeel et al. (2023) found perfectionism and a fear of being unable to complete tasks
amongst accounting students, who prioritised immediate competence over the learning
process, with one student quoting: "I am quite a perfectionist. I first need to review
everything... before starting".
Eiselen and Geyser (2004) and Gracia and Jenkins (2002) found that at-risk accounting
students found public sharing of test results humiliating and felt intense fear and
embarrassment when answering questions in class. These indicate heightened sensitivity to
22

--- Page 23 ---

how peers might view their performance.
These types of avoidance may be interpreted as low confidence; however, the MMS suggests
that the desire to maintain the perception of intelligence may influence these behaviours.
6.2 The Academic Environment Shapes Goal Orientation
Students’ personal goal orientations may be overridden by the learning environment.
Accounting curricula may unintentionally signal performance-oriented expectations, which
can lead even growthers to shift toward a performance goal orientation when the environment
is heavily focused on assessment outcomes (Jackling, 2005; Ravenscroft et al., 2012).
This can work in reverse. Molendijk et al. (2024) reported that ‘assessment’ was rarely
mentioned as accounting students’ reason for engagement or disengagement in the
Experiential Learning Activities (ELAs) included in the study. Students were informed that
the ELAs were excluded from formal assessments, suggesting that, in an environment where
the activity’s purpose was explicitly framed as learning rather than performance, students’
focus shifted accordingly.
Performance goals are not universally detrimental and can sometimes lead to high immediate
marks through ‘exam tricks’ or strategic focus on existing strengths (Cha et al., 2025). In a
study of additional accounting academic support programmes, Ontong, De Waal, and Wentzel
(2020) reported that 'assistance with examination technique' was the dominant student
objective (above 45 percent), overshadowing 'course content' (approximately 30 percent) and
'general advice on study approach to successfully complete my studies' (less than 10 percent).
The stark contrast between the short-term ‘exam tips’ and the advice for longer-term success
suggests performance goal orientation.
Given the students’ positive feedback for the support programmes as a whole, their
23

--- Page 24 ---

short-term objective prioritisation may be driven by the urgency and emphasis on passing
each academic year for qualification, and is concerning because of the context of this study -
low pass rates for exit-level exams for accounting students.
To understand why a student would feel compelled to prioritise validation over learning, even
when it impedes their progress, we must look to the core assumption that drives the entire
psychological engine. The MMS suggests that a student's effort belief and goal orientation are
predicted by their implicit belief of intelligence.
7. Implicit Beliefs (Fixed and Growth Mindsets)
We have traced the pipeline leak from the observable outcome (helpless response) through
the elements that dictate that response (effort beliefs and goal orientation). We now arrive at
the foundation of the MMS: the Implicit Belief. This core belief—whether intelligence is a
fixed trait or a malleable quality—sets the entire meaning system in motion (Dweck &
Leggett, 1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019; Cha et al., 2025; Burnette et al., 2013).
Given the paper's focus on the helpless responses driving the pipeline leak, the following
discussion centres on the specific belief system that the MMS identifies as the precursor to
those responses: the fixed mindset.
7.1 Mindset in Accounting Education Research
As noted in the methodology, empirical research explicitly measuring implicit beliefs in
accounting is limited. The current literature suggests that accounting student populations
exhibit exceptionally high growth mindset ratios, ranging from 72 percent to 80 percent
(Ravenscroft et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2018; Beatson et al., 2019; Farok & Mahmud, 2020).
These figures are significantly higher than those in broader psychological research, which
typically finds more balanced distributions (Blackwell et al., 2007; Claro, Paunesku, &
24

--- Page 25 ---

Dweck, 2016; Mofield & Parker Peters, 2018).
If taken at face value, the high growth-mindset ratios would refute the suggestion that at-risk
accounting students may be likely to respond helplessly to challenges. It may also explain
why the field hasn't investigated mindset theory further.
Along with the inflated ratios, the impact of these reported mindsets on academic
performance is inconsistent. While Liu et al. (2018) and Farok and Mahmud (2020) found a
positive relationship, Beatson et al. (2019) found no correlation, and Ravenscroft et al. (2012)
reported mixed results.
This section argues that these inflated ratios and inconsistent results are likely not a reflection
of accounting students' true beliefs, but a consequence of sample composition and the
absence of failure.
7.2 The Importance of Failure as a Trigger
For early mindset studies, students were given tasks that were challenging yet matched their
ability and competence (manageable challenges) (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Diener & Dweck,
1978, 1980; Hong et al., 1999). In response to these challenges, most students displayed
growth-mindset responses. Researchers then provided them with tasks designed to be
impossible (unmanageable challenges), and, as illustrated in Figure 2, these challenges
triggered a shift from growth- to fixed-mindset narratives for some students. I refer to ‘failure
triggers’ to represent the unmanageable challenge that causes the shift from growther to
fixeder for some students.
Failure triggers may be real or perceived, may represent actual failure in assessments, or may
be perceived setbacks during the academic period, such as struggles or overwhelming
challenges the student considers impossible to overcome.
25

--- Page 26 ---

This is important for the pipeline leak paradox, which explains why students can successfully
navigate earlier levels but then struggle later on. The difference between successfully
navigated challenges and those at the exit level may lie in whether the student sees them as
manageable or unmanageable (Figure 2; Table 2).
Without a failure trigger, mindset research suggests that a fixeder may appear
indistinguishable from a growther (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Diener & Dweck, 1978, 1980;
Hong et al., 1999). They are performing well, receiving validation, and thus have no reason to
question their ability or articulate a belief that it is limited. They may encounter challenges,
but they believe they are in control of the solution and can overcome them.

7.3 Fixeder Profile in Accounting Education
Mindset research suggests that high-achieving students are more likely to receive praise for
their results rather than their processes, a pattern linked to the development of fixed beliefs,
especially at school levels where learning beliefs are formed (Mueller & Dweck, 1988;
Kamins & Dweck, 1999). Given that entry into accounting programmes typically requires
strong academic performance, accounting students are likely to fit this profile. Unless
feedback is consciously structured to value effort, the dominant message students absorb is
that success—defined by ease, speed, and correctness—is what counts (Dweck & Leggett,
1988; Dweck & Yeager, 2019; Cha et al., 2025).
In the absence of mindset studies in accounting education that specifically include at-risk
accounting students, we can instead look to accounting education literature for parallels
between students’ reported narratives and the traits mindset theory associates with fixed
beliefs: Identity-based beliefs and the belief that intelligence and performance are outside of
their control (Dweck & Yeager, 2019; Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Diener & Dweck, 1978,
1980; Burnette et al., 2013; Cha et al., 2025).

7.3.1 Identity-Based Beliefs
In fixed-mindset frameworks, beliefs about intelligence are often entwined with personal
identity. A growther’s response to failure is “I failed”, while a fixeder’s response is “I am a
failure” (Dweck, 2006; Hong et al., 1999).
Fixed-mindset narratives are evident in Fisher et al.’s (2024) study on burnout among
accounting majors, with students commonly endorsing statements such as “If I fail at school,
I am a failure as a person.” Failing accounting students in Gracia and Jenkins’s (2002) study
described their performance in trait-like, stable terms (“I have a bad memory”; “I don’t
deserve to be on the course”; “I always struggle to concentrate”).

(Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Burnette et al., 2013). Such beliefs turn academic performance into
a test of who the student is, not simply what they know (Kamins & Dweck, 1999). This
heightens evaluative pressure and helps explain the prevalence of anxiety and disengagement

7.3.2 Locus of Control
Fixeders equate success with getting things right quickly and smoothly. When challenges
arise, they may interpret them not as opportunities to grow but as signals that they have
reached their intellectual limit (Dweck & Leggett, 1988; Dweck, 2006; Dweck & Yeager,
2019; Cha et al., 2025). Thus. fixeders believe performance is outside their control (whether
due to internal, unchangeable traits or external circumstances), while growthers see it as
changeable through effort.
Evidence of this attributional pattern appears in accounting education research, with Van den
Berg and Rothmann (2024) describing accounting students’ lack of agency as
‘disempowered’. The negative assessments of teaching quality in Jackling (2005) and Natoli
et al.’s (2020) studies confirmed the authors’ concerns that students’ sense of responsibility
for learning is externalised. Student survey responses emphasised lecturers’ responsibility to
motivate, explain clearly, provide feedback, and make classes interesting, suggesting that
students perceive their performance is determined by external factors.
Similarly, in both Gracia and Jenkins’ (2002) and Eiselen and Geyser’s (2004) studies, failing
students attributed poor performance to factors outside their control, such as teaching quality,
workload, and institutional conditions, suggesting that the university improve these elements.
Khan et al. (2023) drew a specific mindset analogy to accounting dropouts, suggesting that
students withdraw from professional qualifications (ACCA and Chartered Accountants (CA))
due to actual or pre-emptive failure, driven by the fixed-mindset belief that circumstances are
beyond their influence.
These findings and student narratives in the above studies align with fixed-mindset
narratives. Further research, perhaps incorporating more recent scales used in STEM mindset
research (Limeri, 2025), may yield valuable insights.

7. Conclusion
Given the paradox of accounting students who, despite years of rigorous preparation,
encounter difficulties at higher levels of their qualification journey, this paper draws on
mindset research to provide a framework for understanding these struggles.
By applying the Mindset Meaning System (MMS) as a reverse-diagnostic lens, this analysis
suggests that this attrition is not indicative of knowledge or ability deficits, but rather a result
of helpless responses to perceived unmanageable challenges encountered at higher levels of
the qualification process. Reversing the traditional MMS sequence reveals a coherent
psychological trajectory for the at-risk accounting student.
The MMS suggests that implicit beliefs predict students’ goal orientation and effort beliefs,
which in turn dictate their responses to setbacks and failure. Given the types and levels of
challenges at higher levels, these responses are reasonably expected to manifest more, as
increased pressure would exacerbate them.
The MMS is not a separate cause of student failure, nor does it 'create' disengagement or
anxiety in a vacuum. Rather, it serves as a foundational logic that makes these
concepts—such as disengagement or help-seeking—more or less likely, depending on the
student’s mindset. It suggests that for some students, a helpless response is not a lack of will,
but a logical conclusion drawn from a belief that effort has reached its limit.
Helpless responses are not a distinct cognitive-psychological area; they merely describe
various responses that are not appropriate for addressing the challenges faced, and are
detrimental to students’ well-being and potential for success.
What I have illustrated is that, if current accounting research is viewed through the lens of the
MMS, we may be able to understand the types of people, beliefs, and behaviours that underlie
31

--- Page 32 ---

some of the attrition we find at higher levels of the accounting qualification, and why
traditional study advice may not be effective.
By reinterpreting existing accounting research through this lens, this paper demonstrates that
fragmented findings—such as surface learning or externalised responsibility—may actually
be symptoms of a single, interconnected meaning system.
Reframing this literature through the MMS highlights a critical gap: current research often
captures 'untested optimism' by sampling successful students. It would be valuable to explore
the 'stress-tested' beliefs of at-risk learners in future accounting research. Without including
those who have experienced genuine failure, we cannot determine if the attrition at the exit
level is a matter of technical skill or a collapse of the student's meaning system under
pressure.
Ultimately, the MMS does not replace existing accounting education frameworks; it
strengthens them. It connects fragmented findings, identifies blind spots in research design,
and provides a theoretically grounded pathway to support the students who are most
committed to the profession yet most vulnerable to its final challenges and further research
into each of these elements, as well as the MMS as a whole, may provide valuable insights
for educators.
8. Limitations
This paper is a conceptual analysis rather than a causal study, and its primary limitation stems
from the scarcity of mindset-specific literature in accounting. Consequently, the
reinterpretation of non-mindset studies (Methodology - Phase 2) should be viewed as
theoretically suggestive. While grounded in the MMS and clear behavioural parallels, these
findings remain inferences.
32

--- Page 33 ---

Furthermore, the lack of binary performance stratification in existing accounting research
(e.g., pass or fail) limits the precision with which helpless versus mastery-oriented patterns
can be distinguished among at-risk cohorts.
Finally, the reliance on qualitative accounts necessitates including studies of varying ages and
journal rankings, reflecting the limited availability of narrative-rich data in the field rather
than a methodological preference.
