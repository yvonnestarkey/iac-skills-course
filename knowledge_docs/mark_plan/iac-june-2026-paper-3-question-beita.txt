1 Introduction
Dino Biko and Siphiwe Keita, who have known each other since completing their interior design
studies at a leading tertiary institution, have always had a dream of giving back to the community.
They decided that a not-for-profit organisation (NPO), in the form of a trust, would be the best
vehicle for their venture. They set up Beita Projects Trust (‘Beita Projects’) towards the end of
2021 and registered it with the South African Revenue Service (SARS) as a public benefit
organisation (PBO), as that would grant it tax-exempt status. However, with its focus on
community development, a condition of an NPO is that Beita Projects’ assets and resources
should be used only to achieve the objectives for which it was created. Furthermore, while the
activities of Beita Projects do not need to have a profit motive, any profits it generates should be
used to further the organisation’s objectives.
Beita Projects operated as an NPO for three years. It focused on the upskilling and development
of the community through the manufacture of furniture with a South African theme using cheap
wood. Following the success of Beita Projects, its operations were subsequently transferred to
Beita Furniture (Pty) Ltd (‘Beita Furniture’) on 1 January 2025. This private company was
incorporated on the transfer date. Like Beita Projects, Beita Furniture is a local manufacturer, and
it continued to produce furniture with distinct South African designs.
Beita Projects, and subsequently Beita Furniture, are both located in Coega, an economic
development zone (EDZ) in the Eastern Cape and are members of the Coega Development
Corporation (CDC). Economic development zones assist in the expansion of commercial and
industrial businesses and long-term employment. The CDC supports businesses and
organisations that operate from the EDZ through incentives offered by the national government
and the local municipality. These include training, research and development and promotion of
export incentives. Customs duty relief and employment tax incentives are also part of the possible
incentives offered by the CDC to entities that meet strict development criteria.
2 Background on Beita Projects
Dino and Siphiwe raised R1 million through donations to fund the start-up of Beita Projects.
Kunomoya Enterprises, one of the main participants in the production incentive programme at
Coega, offered Beita Projects 200 m2 of unused factory space in its warehouse at a total rental
cost of R500 per month. A market-related rate for rent of similar premises is R150 per m2.
Beita Projects purchased good quality second-hand carpentry equipment at a liquidation auction
for only R250 000. Other operating costs were funded with donations received from various
companies.
Fulani Bhule and Joseph Nnadi are qualified carpenters who joined the Beita Projects team at
inception. Fulani has 20 years carpentry experience. Joseph was a supervisor at the company he
worked for previously, where he was involved in product pricing and customer relations. Fulani
and Joseph were employed to coordinate Beita Projects’ work with trainee carpenters. Beita
Projects arranged to pay Joseph and Fulani fixed salaries of R15 000 per month each, until the
sale of furniture could cover the operational costs of the NPO. Thereafter, they would be paid
market-related monthly salaries of R25 000 and R27 000, respectively.

Starting from November 2021, Beita Projects, through its community upskilling programme, used
the making of furniture to help unemployed persons learn new skills as trainee carpenters. The
proceeds made from the sale of the furniture were used to compensate the trainee carpenters.
The trainee carpenters qualify as entry-level carpenters at the end of their training, which enables
them to pursue opportunities in the carpentry industry. By the end of 2024, 14 individuals had
completed the programme and moved on to other opportunities.

3.2 Pallets
When it opened in November 2021, Beita Projects had a launch day at which the local community
could drop off any unwanted wood and wooden items at its premises. For six months thereafter,
Fulani and Joseph used this wood to teach the employees basic carpentry skills and how to use
the various items of equipment. The wood was used to make small household items. During this
initial period, Beita Projects used R250 000 of the start-up donations.
The donated wood was either not sufficient or unsuitable for furniture production, and hence
Joseph researched cost-effective alternatives. The most viable option was second-hand pallets,
which are readily available and could be used to make outdoor furniture after being treated.
Competitors manufacture high-quality tables and benches from plastic materials or railway
sleeper wood that can withstand the environment better than wooden pallets.
Joseph approached nearby businesses to share Beita Projects’ vision and asked whether he
could buy second-hand pallets at R10 per pallet, which was considerably lower than the average
market-related price of R80. Many of the businesses had used pallets that they were willing to
sell at Joseph’s proposed price, on condition that Beita Projects collect the pallets. Joseph
borrowed a truck at no cost from Kunomoya Enterprises to pick up the pallets weekly.

Joseph managed to collect 850 pallets at R10 per pallet in the first week, with regular collections
of pallets thereafter. Joseph and Fulani started with the production of two standard items, being
tables and benches. The 850 pallets would theoretically be sufficient to make 150 three-seater
outdoor benches and 120 outdoor tables, assuming a standard usage of 4,5 pallets per table (of
which half a pallet was lost as waste) and two pallets per bench (10% wastage on the two pallets
used). All pallets are of a standard size and consist of 20 planks per pallet. All planks are utilised
in the production process, subject to the normal wastage. To limit wastage, pallets with broken
planks are used for training purposes only.
3.3 Trainee carpenters
From May 2023 onwards, Joseph and Fulani trained ten employees at a time. For the first half of
each day employees were trained to make household items. Joseph and Fulani spent the second
half of the day supervising them while they made components for tables and benches. There is
no fixed period for trainees to complete their training. As the skills of the ten employees improved,
the quality of their work and volumes produced also improved.
From May 2024 the programme operated at expected capacity and the employees consisted, at
any given stage, of ten staff who had completed their training, plus five individuals who were still
in training.
The trainees are paid R3 200 per month (R20 per hour) and are expected to work eight hours per
day and on average 20 days per month. Once they are operating at expected capacity and have
completed their training, their monthly pay increases to R4 000 per month (R25 per hour).
3.4 Treatment of wood
Dino and Siphiwe are not involved in the day-to-day operations of the furniture production. When
they visited the production warehouse during October 2024, they asked Joseph to prepare
management accounts. Joseph’s analysis for the three months ending 31 October 2024 is set out
in appendix 1.
After inspecting Joseph’s analysis and consulting with a furniture expert in the hotel industry, Dino
and Siphiwe predicted that these tables and/or benches could easily be sold to hotel groups and
wine estates.
However, this would require that someone with more experience in treating and properly finishing
the furniture be employed. Joseph recommended someone who could perform this function for
R12 000 per month. This person would spend 75% of their time operating the machines, treating
planks and finishing the furniture, and the balance on the training programme teaching the
trainees the necessary skills.
Beita Projects also needed to acquire a specialised machine that could be used only for treating
the planks. Three of these machines were leased on 1 November 2024 for a period of one year
at a total fixed rental of R72 000 per annum, payable in equal monthly instalments, plus a charge
of R15 per machine hour used. The machines would not be used for training purposes, and each
has a capacity of 200 hours per month.
Planks that are extensively exposed to sunlight require 15 minutes of treatment and finishing,
while the planks that are not subject to extensive exposure only require five minutes of treatment.

4 Incorporation as a private company, NPO status and the expansion of Beita
Furniture
Dino started negotiating with two boutique hotels on supplying tables and benches to them. These
hotels are part of the Swallows Group, which also owns a wine farm in the Cape winelands. In
view of the interest of the Swallows Group and other wine estates, Dino and Siphiwe decided to
incorporate a private company to continue with the business. That meant, however, that the
operation of Beita Projects as an NPO would need to be discontinued, as an NPO cannot merely
be converted to a private company.
Consequently, they incorporated a new private company, Beita Furniture (Pty) Ltd, on
1 January 2025. The directors decided to retain the Beita name by calling the new company Beita
Furniture to ensure the donors remained aware of the Beita concept and its aims and objectives.
Beita Furniture’s first financial year end was 31 December 2025 (FY2025). It was incorporated
with 150 000 authorised shares. Dino and Siphiwe were each issued 37 500 shares at
incorporation. According to the company’s memorandum of incorporation (MoI), Dino and Siphiwe
are the only two directors of Beita Furniture. Dino acts as chief executive officer and Siphiwe fulfils
the role of chief operations officer.
Dino and Siphiwe transferred all assets and liabilities to the new company at book value and the
net asset value was credited to the initial share capital of the company. It was resolved that any
future funding requirements would be in the form of bank loans. Dino and Siphiwe kept and
approved minutes of all their meetings. Fortunately, despite its change in status, many of Beita
Projects’ supporters continued making donations towards Beita Furniture’s operating expenses.
At a meeting held on 3 July 2025, Dino and Siphiwe decided to issue 12 500 shares each to
Joseph and Fulani at a nominal consideration of R0,01 per share, as they were seen to be key
employees and critical to the success of the company. The shares were issued on 31 July 2025.
The directors decided that 50% of the profits of the company would be shared amongst staff
members who had been employed by Beita Furniture for more than six months. The profit share
for a quarter would be paid in cash in the month following the end of the quarter. Staff would not
share in any losses.
On 4 December 2025, Dino and Siphiwe discussed the possibility of paying out a small dividend
to the four shareholders. They drew up a schedule of dividends, performed a solvency test, and
concluded that a dividend of R0,20 per share was feasible. They approved the dividend payouts
accordingly and kept minutes of this approval for future reference. The dividends were paid on
30 December 2025.

5 Beita Furniture’s financial statement audit and internal control matters
5.1 Beita Furniture’s FY2025 financial statement audit
To qualify for a bank loan (for additional financing), Dino approached Plank & Partners (‘PP’), a
medium-sized accounting and audit firm located in Gqeberha, to audit Beita Furniture’s financial
statements for FY2025. This was in line with the MoI, which also requires that the company be
audited. The financial statements should also comply with the requirements of the IFRS®
Accounting Standards. PP specialises in the financial services sector, and it was excited about
expanding its portfolio of clients to include Beita Furniture, a manufacturing company. Fezile Keita
CA(SA) RA, an audit partner at PP, was assigned to the audit of Beita Furniture.
Because Fezile is Siphiwe’s brother, the two were comfortable about sharing information with one
another and felt that this would lead to a seamless audit process. There would be one other team
member on the audit, namely Meagan Brooks, a second-year trainee accountant, who would take
ultimate responsibility for the audit as she would be performing all audit procedures. Fezile would
provide her with strategic direction in performing the audit and ultimately sign the audit report.
5.2 Internal controls and the finance function of Beita Furniture
On 1 April 2025, Zolani Ndungane was appointed to perform the bookkeeping and costing
functions, implement internal controls and perform day-to-day financial functions at Beita
Furniture. Zolani has recently completed her BCom degree. Because she does not have any work
experience, Dino and Siphiwe were able to appoint her at a very low salary for a candidate with
her qualifications.
Beita Furniture outsourced the preparation of tax returns and the compilation of the financial
statements for FY2025 to PP.
6 Other information
6.1 Alternative pallet supplier
Dino’s boyfriend introduced her to a local businessman who said he could deliver at least 500
new pallets at a cost of R40 per pallet, plus a R20 commission per pallet payable to her boyfriend.
Dino accepted the proposal and the first sample batch of ten pallets was delivered to Beita
Furniture’s premises. Dino noted that the name of a big transport company was painted on the
pallets. Her boyfriend told her that the pallets were ‘excess to requirements’ and the businessman
was getting them as a special deal.
6.2 Human resources
As Beita Furniture does not have a human resources department, Zolani made it her priority to
ensure that the necessary appointment documents were in place for all employees. Zolani could
not find an employment contract for one of the trainee carpenters, Abulele Makwetu. Zolani sent
him a WhatsApp message to confirm whether he had a contract or not.
